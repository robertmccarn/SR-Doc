# Geopolitical forecast research and multi-factor integration, 2024–2040

**Status:** Research synthesis and non-canon proposal input.
**Maintainer direction:** Deemphasize climate as the primary catalyst. Integrate political, demographic, supply-chain, AI/technology, energy, military, economic, institutional, social, and environmental avenues for change.
**Realism constraint:** No departure from authentic real-world realism through 2040-12-31.
**Branch:** `experiment/corrected-realism`
**Research cutoff:** 2026-11-25.
**Primary integration target:** [Realism-first continuity baseline and replacement timeline, 2024–2040](realism-first-timeline-2024-2040.md).

## 1. Executive synthesis

The most useful contribution of George Friedman, Stratfor, and Geopolitical Futures is not a catalogue of events to copy into the setting. It is a way to think about how geography, economic structure, demographics, military capability, technology, political institutions, and neighboring powers constrain the choices available to states.

The RANE/Stratfor Center's 2025–2035 framing is especially compatible with the maintainer's requested approach: a multipolar system driven by national self-interest, unilateralism, weakening traditional blocs, and more influential middle powers; it explicitly treats AI, climate change, energy transition, space, and cyberspace as separate strategic factors. It also cautions that a decade forecast is intended to identify broad patterns and pathways, not predict specific events. [Sources 1–3]

The GPF model likewise describes geopolitics as an integrated interaction among geography, economy, politics, military power, technology, culture, and surrounding communities. Its central analytic language is the tension between national imperatives and constraints. [Sources 4–5]

**Editorial conclusion:** The 2024–2040 setting should be shaped by multiple interacting pressures, with no single master catastrophe. Climate remains important, but should not explain political crises, economic disruption, migration, war, technological competition, and institutional change by default. The same event can have different causes in different regions.

## 3. Primary-source findings and implications

### 3.1 Friedman's long-range structural forecasts

**The Next 100 Years (2009).** The central structural claims relevant to this setting include:
- China's growth model would weaken and regional tensions could produce fragmentation.
- Russia would struggle to sustain influence because of demographic weakness and reliance on resource revenues.
- Japan, Turkey, and Poland could gain regional power as the strategic environment changes.
- Aging advanced economies would face labor-force pressures and immigration questions.

Kirkus's contemporary review summarizes the China/Russia fragmentation and Japan/Turkey/Poland rise thesis. [Source 8] These are hypotheses, not established outcomes. As of the 2026 research cutoff, China has not fragmented and Russia has not dissolved. The useful realism-compatible elements are structural stressors—aging, regional inequality, export dependence, resource dependence, strategic geography, and defense capacity—not the predicted collapse dates.

**The Next Decade (2011).** Use this as a source for Friedman's view of U.S. strategic constraints and the choices facing American leaders. The publisher's description confirms the book's focus on the decade's leaders and U.S. foreign policy challenges, but detailed claims should be taken from the book itself rather than inferred from its marketing copy. [Source 9]

**Flashpoints (2015).** This is most useful for European borderlands, security dilemmas, historical memory, migration, identity, unemployment, and the fragility of political settlements. The Carnegie Council event transcript records Friedman's argument that European stability after 1992 was an unusual interregnum and that unresolved class and interstate tensions could re-emerge. [Source 10] This can inform European political tension, but it does not justify assuming EU collapse or a new continental war.

**The Storm Before the Calm (2020).** Friedman proposes overlapping 80-year institutional and 50-year socioeconomic cycles converging in the late 2020s. The publisher's description identifies the institutional-cycle analogies (Revolution, Civil War, World War II) and the socioeconomic-cycle argument, including changes in federal scope, corporate structures, social contract, marriage, and responses to longer life expectancy. [Source 11] For the setting, use this as a lens for institutional legitimacy and generational bargaining—not as a deterministic clock, and emphatically not as a prediction that the United States must experience civil war.

### 3.2 GPF's own model and forecast method

GPF describes its model as beginning with geography and then integrating economy, politics, military capability, technology, culture, and surrounding communities. The framework asks what a state must do to survive/prosper and what constraints prevent it from doing so. [Source 4]

Friedman's 2021 description of GPF's annual process says analysts make short, declarative forecasts at the start of November, then review the prior year's predictions before building the next forecast. The article explicitly warns against both safe, unfalsifiable generalities and seeing a pattern that is not really there. [Source 5]

GPF's public archive lists annual forecasts from at least 2016 through 2026 and displays some report cards/trackers. It also provides a summary of *The Road to 2040*. [Source 12] This is useful for a longitudinal forecast register: save the forecast as it appeared before the outcome, then compare it with a dated, measurable outcome.

**The Road to 2040 (original forecast dated December 2, 2015; public summary dated July 15, 2021).** The public summary forecasts:
- Continued U.S. global primacy but a lower-cost, less interventionist strategy and greater reliance on allies.
- A possible EU collapse or reduction to a smaller trade zone, with Germany weakened by export dependence and Poland gaining influence.
- Russian devolution or fragmentation under fiscal/resource pressures, with nuclear security becoming a strategic issue.
- Chinese regionalism and turmoil as export competitiveness and employment weaken.
- Japan gaining a leading East Asian role.
- Turkey becoming a major regional power, with military engagement against extremist groups.
- A more disordered Eurasia contrasted with greater North American stability. [Source 13]

This is a forecast thesis, not a factual description or a license to reproduce its strongest outcomes. It supplies plausible scenario questions: Can the U.S. reduce direct intervention while retaining influence? Can Europe rearm and coordinate without full political unity? Can Poland gain influence while remaining constrained by Russia and EU/NATO institutions? Can China experience slower growth and internal policy tension without state disintegration? Can Japan increase defense capacity while managing demographic constraints? Can Turkey convert geographic opportunity into durable economic and diplomatic leverage without imperial-scale occupation?

**GPF 2023 preliminary long-term forecast.** Friedman stated that GPF was tentatively considering expanding U.S. geopolitical power, the possibility that economic and social pressure could return China to civil strife, and major turmoil in Russia related to Ukraine. He explicitly called these preliminary and said other issues remained only an agenda for analysis. [Source 14] The China civil-strife possibility should be treated as a low-confidence scenario, not the basis for retaining the source corpus's 2027–2048 Chinese civil war. A realistic setting can depict regional policy friction, elite competition, protest, labor and property stress, and center–province tension without a nationwide civil war.

**GPF 2025 Turkey analysis.** In August 2025, Friedman and Kamran Bokhari argued that Turkey had an unusual geopolitical opportunity because of Russian preoccupation in Ukraine, changing U.S. engagement, Iranian weakness, and regional shifts. They pointed to Turkey's defense capacity, the South Caucasus/Zangezur corridor, Syria, Arab diplomacy, Libya, and the Balkans. [Source 15] This is a much more credible mechanism for increasing Turkish influence than an abrupt occupation of Arabia or an implausible Arctic-to-Bosporus pipeline. Influence can expand through corridors, arms, trade, mediation, selective deployments, and partnerships while still facing fiscal, domestic, and regional constraints.

**GPF 2026 forecast.** The public archive's headline is “China has effectively replaced Russia as a global power.” The detailed article is subscriber-only, so this synthesis does not infer the full argument from the headline. [Source 16] It is a useful marker of how GPF's relative-power assessment has evolved since its 2015/2021 forecast of Russian fragmentation and Chinese turmoil. Preserve that change as a forecast evolution, not as proof that either country has collapsed or definitively replaced the other.

### 3.3 Stratfor/RANE institutional forecasts

Stratfor's public decade archive lists forecasts for 1995–2005, 2000–2010, 2005–2015, 2010–2020, 2015–2025, 2020–2030, and 2025–2035. The public index exposes summary language, including:
- 2015–2025: continued U.S. primacy but more cautious external engagement.
- 2020–2030: movement toward multipolarity and more fluid alliances.
- 2025–2035: multipolar competition, with intra-bloc competition as important as East–West competition. [Source 17]

The RANE/Stratfor Center's 2025–2035 forecast describes national self-interest, unilateralism, weakening traditional blocs, and middle-power influence. It explicitly names AI, climate change, energy transition, space, and cyberspace as critical factors. [Sources 1–3] This supports a multi-axis story. It is also a useful reminder that technology and digital infrastructure are geopolitical terrain, not decorative background.

The public RANE Worldview homepage displays quarterly forecasts, but the detailed forecast links are marked premium/subscriber-only. Its public archive and Stratfor Center provide some open reports, webinars, and podcasts. [Sources 7, 18] Current RANE material should be attributed to RANE/Stratfor analysts, not to Friedman.

### 3.4 Independent evaluation and forecast humility

The 2018 *Journal for Economic Forecasting* paper “Stratfor vs. Reality (1995–2025). Dilemmas in Global Forecasting” examines the decade forecasts and the methodological difficulty of evaluating geopolitical forecasts. Its abstract stresses the multiplicity of political, economic, and military variables and the difficulty of comparing predictions across unlike contexts. [Source 19]

The evaluation should not rely on promotional “prediction hit” lists or retrospective anecdotes. Build a claim-level scorecard with:
- the exact original forecast wording and publication date;
- horizon and geographic scope;
- whether it is directional, probabilistic, or a specific event claim;
- an observable success criterion;
- the outcome at the forecast horizon;
- hit / partial hit / inconclusive / partial miss / miss;
- whether the forecast was revised before the horizon;
- the evaluator's evidence and confidence.

A broad statement such as “multipolarity will increase” must not receive a hit merely because several unrelated states became more assertive. A forecast of Russian fragmentation by 2040 is not a hit because Russia experienced a war or sanctions. A forecast of Chinese regionalism is not confirmed by a slowdown alone. A prediction that Turkey will gain influence is not the same as predicting occupation or territorial conquest.

## 4. Multi-factor causal architecture for the 2024–2040 setting

The setting should not have one master catalyst. Treat the following as independent but interacting domains. The same domain can create opportunity for one actor and vulnerability for another.

| Domain | Plausible mechanisms through 2040 | What it can change in the story | Realism guardrail |
|---|---|---|---|
| Geography and strategic position | Chokepoints, ports, rivers, mountain barriers, access to warm-water routes, distance, land borders, maritime exposure | Alliances, basing, trade routes, regional influence, military posture | Geography constrains choices; it does not dictate inevitable conquest. |
| Political institutions and legitimacy | Polarization, executive-legislative conflict, court rulings, federal/state friction, coalition instability, corruption, public trust | Policy continuity, protest, reform, government turnover, selective unrest | Political crisis is not automatically civil war or state collapse. |
| Demographics and migration | Aging, low fertility, urbanization, labor shortages, internal migration, immigration politics, skills mismatch | Pension/health costs, labor bargaining, housing, voting coalitions, recruitment, regional growth/decline | Use official population measures and distinguish temporary displacement from permanent migration. |
| Industrial structure and supply chains | Concentrated refining, semiconductor fabrication, shipping dependence, export controls, single-source components, inventory and logistics shocks | Inflation, production pauses, industrial policy, reshoring, sanctions, strategic stockpiles | Disruptions are sector-specific; substitution and inventories create delays and uneven effects. |
| AI, automation, and digital infrastructure | Data-center demand, compute concentration, chips, power/water use, model deployment, cyber operations, labor substitution, surveillance | Productivity, labor displacement/reorganization, military intelligence, information integrity, infrastructure risk | AI is not autonomous omnipotence; deployment depends on energy, chips, capital, data, institutions, and human operators. |
| Energy and critical minerals | Grid bottlenecks, copper, lithium, rare earths, uranium, gas, oil, refining, storage, export restrictions | Strategic leverage, price shocks, industrial competition, energy security | Mining/refining/transport projects take years; reserves are not the same as production capacity. |
| Finance and fiscal capacity | Sovereign debt, interest costs, pension obligations, insurance retreat, credit cycles, currency pressure, capital flight | Austerity, defense tradeoffs, infrastructure delays, banking stress, recession | A crash needs a defined trigger and transmission path; no deterministic demographic market collapse. |
| Military and security | Rearmament, drone/munition production, cyber conflict, space assets, nuclear deterrence, alliance burden-sharing, gray-zone operations | Arms races, deterrence, proxy competition, local escalation, security guarantees | Military capacity requires training, logistics, munitions, command cohesion, and political will. |
| Trade and institutional order | Bilateral deals, minilateral blocs, sanctions, tariffs, WTO/UN limits, regional institutions, investment screening | Fragmented rules, competing standards, regional arrangements, reduced predictability | Globalization changes shape; it does not simply vanish. |
| Climate, ecology, and water | Heat, drought, flood, wildfire, sea-level rise, crop variability, water stress, insurance exposure | Local adaptation, fiscal pressure, migration, food prices, infrastructure damage | Effects are regional, cumulative, and mediated by wealth, institutions, infrastructure, and preparedness. |
| Public health and biosecurity | Epidemic risk, antimicrobial resistance, health-system capacity, biomanufacturing, biosecurity governance | Labor availability, emergency powers, supply resilience, public trust | Do not assume a pandemic; distinguish plausible preparedness from a forecast event. |
| Culture, information, and social cohesion | Demographic identity debates, religious movements, media fragmentation, disinformation, education gaps, generational conflict | Legitimacy, elections, protest, recruitment, social trust | Avoid treating populations as monoliths; local histories and institutions matter. |
| Science, space, and infrastructure | Robotics, launch services, satellites, advanced materials, nuclear technologies, ports, rail, power grids, undersea cables | Industrial capacity, surveillance, navigation, communications, strategic dependence | Distinguish research, prototype, deployment, and scaled operation. |

### Interaction chains (illustrative, not predetermined)

1. **AI/compute → electricity → minerals → industrial policy → trade conflict.** Data-center and semiconductor expansion increases demand for power, grid equipment, copper, advanced chips, memory, and strategic minerals. Concentrated refining or export restrictions can then become bargaining leverage. The IEA's 2026 critical-minerals assessment explicitly links minerals to AI data centers, robotics, semiconductors, aerospace, and defense, and documents concentration and value-chain bottlenecks. [Source 20]
2. **Aging → fiscal pressure → labor shortages → automation/immigration politics.** Aging affects pension and healthcare costs while reducing labor-force growth. Governments and firms respond through migration policy, later retirement, productivity investment, training, and automation. Political coalitions disagree over who bears the costs.
3. **War/sanctions → shipping/energy/food inputs → inflation → elections and social pressure.** A regional conflict can disrupt fertilizer, fuel, grain, shipping insurance, or critical components. The economic consequences depend on duration, alternative routes, inventories, substitution, and government intervention.
4. **U.S. strategic retrenchment → allied rearmament → domestic fiscal tradeoffs → new regional alignments.** Allies may increase defense spending while disagreeing over procurement, command, industrial capacity, and strategic autonomy. The result is not necessarily NATO collapse.
5. **Climate shock + water governance + weak infrastructure → localized displacement and political bargaining.** The same hazard has different consequences in a wealthy, well-insured region and a fragile, indebted region. Climate is a multiplier and stressor, not a universal root cause.
6. **Cyber/AI disruption → public-service outage → attribution uncertainty → diplomatic escalation.** A real incident may combine criminal activity, state-linked operators, private infrastructure, and imperfect intelligence. Escalation depends on attribution, response options, and political leadership.

## 5. Regional scenario implications (not predictions)

### United States and North America
- Use Friedman's institutional-cycle thesis as a theme for late-2020s legitimacy disputes, generational conflict, changing federal scope, and renegotiation of the social contract.
- Pair it with real constraints: fiscal capacity, federalism, courts, elections, state governments, private capital, labor markets, immigration, energy production, and alliance commitments.
- The most plausible national transformation is uneven policy adjustment and coalition realignment, not the Second American Civil War or wholesale seizure of housing.
- North American stability can coexist with intense domestic politics, border friction, industrial competition, and selective foreign-policy retrenchment.

### Europe
- Use *Flashpoints* and GPF/RANE forecasts to make European security a product of historical borderlands, energy dependence, export exposure, defense capacity, migration, and competing national interests.
- Germany can face industrial/export and fiscal pressure without becoming a failed state. Poland can gain relative military and diplomatic weight while remaining embedded in EU/NATO and constrained by geography.
- EU reform, differentiated integration, bilateral/minilateral deals, and arguments over defense burden-sharing are more credible than a sudden institutional disappearance.
- The Ukraine war's duration, outcome, and postwar security architecture should shape the region; no exact settlement is assumed here.

### Russia and the post-Soviet space
- Model Russia's strategic depth, resource revenues, sanctions, demographic constraints, military losses, elite cohesion, federal structure, and nuclear deterrent as interacting variables.
- A weaker Russia may remain dangerous and territorially intact. Internal strain, elite conflict, succession uncertainty, and regional bargaining do not equal imminent dissolution.
- Nuclear command-and-control concerns should be handled through arms control, intelligence, diplomacy, safeguards, and crisis planning. A NATO/UN ground operation to seize stockpiles is not a realism-compatible default.
- Central Asia and the Caucasus have agency; they are not merely a vacuum for Russia, Turkey, China, or the West to fill.

### China and East Asia
- Use slowing growth, property/debt problems, aging, youth employment, export competition, technology controls, energy and food security, regional inequality, and elite politics as sources of tension.
- Regionalism or policy decentralization is a possible scenario; a nationwide civil war or disintegration by 2040 is not the default.
- Japan can increase defense capacity and strategic autonomy while facing demographic and fiscal constraints. Its trajectory is not an automatic restoration of empire.
- Taiwan, maritime routes, semiconductor supply chains, U.S. alliances, and regional arms production create credible escalation pathways without requiring total war.

### Turkey, Middle East, and Eastern Mediterranean
- Treat Turkey as a potentially more influential middle power through geography, defense industry, trade corridors, diplomacy, selective intervention, and relations with Azerbaijan, the South Caucasus, Syria, Arab states, Libya, and the Balkans.
- Balance this with inflation, currency vulnerability, domestic political competition, energy dependence, military capacity limits, and the interests of neighboring states.
- Iran, Israel, Gulf states, Egypt, Iraq, Kurdish actors, and non-state groups retain independent interests. The region is not a chessboard on which Turkey can simply restore an empire.
- A corridor such as Zangezur or a trans-Caspian route requires agreements, security, financing, infrastructure, and time; it does not automatically imply sovereignty transfer.

### Africa, Latin America, and the Global South
- Do not let a great-power narrative erase local politics. Debt, food and fuel import dependence, minerals, demographics, urbanization, coups, insurgencies, ports, and external financing interact differently by country.
- Middle powers and regional organizations can bargain among larger powers, but their choices are constrained by domestic legitimacy, infrastructure, currency, and security.
- Critical-mineral supply chains create opportunities and coercive vulnerabilities. Resource endowment alone does not produce prosperity; refining, power, transport, governance, and investment matter.

## 7. Revised narrative architecture for 2024–2040

Use the existing year-by-year chronology as a chronology of overlapping pressures, not a single domino chain. Each year should include at least two or three independent domains, and no one domain should dominate the entire decade.

- **2024–2026:** The world absorbs pandemic aftereffects, wars and sanctions, inflation and interest-rate adjustment, industrial policy, AI/compute investment, energy transition bottlenecks, and climate-related regional losses. These pressures are concurrent; none alone explains the whole system.
- **2027–2029:** Political and institutional responses become more visible. Election outcomes, fiscal constraints, defense burden-sharing, migration politics, industrial subsidies, AI labor impacts, cyber incidents, and supply-chain concentration can reinforce or counteract each other. A U.S. institutional crisis may be a central theme, but does not imply civil war.
- **2030–2032:** The consequences of earlier choices become clearer: aging and labor-force constraints, pension/health costs, automation, power and grid buildout, critical-mineral competition, regional security alignments, and contested trade regimes. Some states adapt; others stagnate.
- **2033–2035:** Uneven adaptation produces regional winners and losers. Europe debates integration and defense; China manages economic and demographic constraints; Japan adjusts defense posture; Turkey exploits regional openings selectively; the United States negotiates its role and domestic social contract.
- **2036–2040:** The international order is more transactional and technologically contested, but not uniformly collapsed. AI and automation are more deeply embedded; power, compute, minerals, skilled labor, and trusted supply chains remain strategic assets. Regional wars and crises remain possible, but their outcomes are contingent and bounded by state capacity and deterrence.

This architecture preserves a world that changes dramatically without requiring an impossible global catastrophe. The narrative can remain high-stakes through uneven growth, institutional distrust, economic coercion, proxy conflict, technology dependence, contested migration, and local disasters.

### Recommended forecast ledger schema

For each forecast claim, store:
- `forecast_id` (stable identifier);
- `publisher` (Friedman / Stratfor / RANE / GPF / other);
- `author` and `attribution_type` (personal, team, institutional);
- `publication_date`, `forecast_horizon_start`, `forecast_horizon_end`;
- `source_title`, `source_url`, `source_access_status` (public, summary-only, subscriber, archived);
- `verbatim_claim` (short excerpt only), `normalized_claim`, `geography`, `domain`;
- `claim_type` (directional trend, conditional scenario, specific event, numeric projection);
- `confidence_as_stated`, `measurable_success_criterion`, `baseline_at_publication`;
- `outcome_observation_date`, `outcome_evidence`, `score` (hit/partial/inconclusive/partial miss/miss);
- `revision_history`, `reviewer_notes`, `confidence_in_score`.

Freeze a forecast version at publication. If the publisher revises it, create a new record; do not overwrite the original. Score only after the stated horizon, and use an explicit intermediate status when the horizon has not elapsed.

### Collection workflow

1. Crawl public forecast indexes and RSS metadata; save canonical URL, title, publication date, author, and access status.
2. Snapshot the original public page and, where allowed, a PDF/Wayback capture with a content hash.
3. Extract forecast claims into atomic, testable statements; do not turn an essay's background analysis into a prediction unless it is explicitly forecast.
4. Preserve personal/institutional attribution. A GPF team forecast is not automatically Friedman-authored; RANE/Stratfor forecasts after his departure are not his personal views.
5. Record contemporaneous baseline data and measurable criteria before scoring.
6. Review annually after the horizon. Record partial/inconclusive status rather than forcing binary success.
7. Compare multiple forecasting traditions and primary data. No single private forecaster should control the setting's geopolitical model.

## 9. Integration rules for future canon proposals

1. **Multi-causal, not monocausal:** no global transformation may be explained primarily by climate unless the specific event is directly climate-driven. Other domains must be independently considered.
2. **Geography as constraint, not destiny:** strategic location shapes incentives but does not guarantee conquest, collapse, or regional dominance.
3. **Demographic pressure is slow-moving:** aging, fertility, and migration affect labor, fiscal policy, military recruitment, housing, and politics over years, not overnight.
4. **Technology requires an industrial base:** compute, chips, electricity, cooling, minerals, talent, manufacturing, cybersecurity, and capital constrain AI and automation.
5. **Supply-chain shocks are specific:** name the component/material, supplier concentration, route, inventory buffer, substitution options, and downstream sectors.
6. **Middle powers have agency:** Poland, Turkey, Japan, India, Brazil, Gulf states, and regional actors can shape events without becoming implausible superpowers.
7. **Forecasts are scenario inputs, not canon evidence:** a forecast from Friedman, GPF, or RANE may inspire a plausible branch, but it cannot establish that an event occurred or will occur.
8. **Avoid forecast laundering:** do not use an analyst's prediction as independent evidence for the same prediction's plausibility. Validate with official statistics, primary government documents, scientific assessments, and independent scholarship.
9. **Keep the 2041 divergence gate:** no extraordinary departure from real-world plausibility before 2041.
10. **Preserve competing scenarios:** for major actors, retain at least one adaptation/resilience path and one deterioration/escalation path. Do not select collapse merely because it is narratively dramatic.

### Important source limitations

- Several GPF and RANE full forecasts are subscriber-only; only their public summaries, metadata, or headlines were reviewed here.
- *The Next 100 Years*, *The Next Decade*, *Flashpoints*, and *The Storm Before the Calm* are copyrighted books. This note uses bibliographic records, public interviews/reviews, and short summaries; it does not claim a full-text audit of every book.
- The 2018 Stratfor evaluation paper predates completion of the 2025 forecast horizon. Its title's 1995–2025 range is a study frame, not proof that all forecasts were scored through 2025 in that paper.
- The 2026 GPF forecast is included as a published company headline; its detailed subscriber-only argument was not reviewed. Do not infer details beyond the public headline.
- The RANE/Stratfor 2025–2035 report itself is behind a lead-capture form; this synthesis uses RANE's public overview and public article, not the gated full report.
- This is a research integration pass, not a complete quantitative forecast accuracy study and not a complete country-by-country scenario assessment.
