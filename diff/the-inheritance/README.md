## Purpose and governing premise

*The Inheritance* follows three generations of the Mercer family from 2026 to 2066. Its central mechanism is institutional inheritance: each generation makes a reasonable decision under immediate pressure; safeguards are weakened, repurposed, or forgotten; later generations inherit the consequences.

The story should reveal legal and institutional history through family moments, physical artifacts, and interpersonal friction rather than exposition. The Mercers are not a conspiratorial dynasty. Their decisions are understandable in context, and their cumulative consequences are larger than any one person's intentions.

The narrative must adapt to the established SR-Doc chronology. It must not alter established dates or make David responsible for the 2051 Japanese attack on the US orbital defense network.

## Master institutional and historical chronology

- **2026 (November):** Recognizable baseline world; regional trade friction and AI-related labor shifts. Thanksgiving gathering at Arthur and Helen's northern Michigan lake house.
- **2028:** Arthur drafts the Columbus Municipal Infrastructure Easement. It contains strict performance metrics, public reporting, municipal inspection rights, rate caps, and a mandatory ten-year legislative renewal review. During the 2030s and 2040s, successor administrations neglect inspections and convert the renewal clause into automatic rollover.
- **2035 (February):** Regional North American energy and grid-component crunch. Claire drafts narrow, time-limited emergency procurement and continuity waivers. Later agencies copy the boilerplate while stripping out sunset and audit clauses.
- **2041:** Beginning of deliberate geopolitical divergence through connected regional crises and shifting security alignments. This is not an instantaneous global collapse.
- **2044:** David signs an IP licensing agreement in Denver categorizing neural-interface telemetry streams as proprietary industrial diagnostics rather than personal medical records. The contract concerns classification and rights in system telemetry; it does not confer ownership of a person or unrestricted control over an implant.
- **2051 (November 23)–2055 (January 14):** World War III, triggered by the Japanese attack on the US orbital defense network. Preserve the distinct sequence: ceasefire **November 22, 2054**; Turkish surrender **January 2, 2055**; Treaty of Geneva **January 14, 2055**.
- **2057:** Negotiation of the proposed **Infrastructure Cooperation Agreement**, a distinct postwar development initiative and separate from the 2057 Turkey peace treaty. Claire reuses the logic of delegated authority to establish predictable rules for private-public orbital and lunar investment.
- **2058–2059:** Participating states and operators consolidate the 2057 agreements into the proposed **Cislunar and Orbital Development Authority (CODA)**, a delegated coordinating body without territorial sovereignty. The Augment Rights Movement emerges in the late 2050s.
- **2061–2071:** Established broader Augment Rights Movement period. Its major political phase is visible by the early 2060s. Arthur dies in 2061.
- **2062:** Lunar industrial economy exists but remains dependent on terrestrial logistics, technically demanding, expensive, and not sovereign by default.
- **2065:** Shackleton Rim dispute erupts over consortium consolidation, medical-data access, employment jurisdiction, worker autonomy, and automated life-support throttling.
- **2066:** Proposed Geneva Tribunal ruling finds that CODA exceeded its delegated jurisdiction in enforcing the consolidation mandate; parties negotiate the proposed Tycho Charter.

## Institutional lineage: proposed, not canon

1. **Treaty of Geneva (January 14, 2055):** Ends WWIII and provides a postwar framework. It does not directly create CODA or a lunar government.
2. **Infrastructure Cooperation Agreement (2057):** A separate agreement among participating governments, aerospace operators, finance institutions, and infrastructure providers. It establishes shared rules for orbital/lunar development, investment, standards, and liability while preserving national sovereignty.
3. **CODA (2058–2059):** The Cislunar and Orbital Development Authority emerges from the 2057 agreement chain. Its authority is delegated by treaty, contract, and participating jurisdictions. Its remit coordinates infrastructure standards/interoperability, shared transport/logistics, safety and emergency response, investment/cost-sharing, and operator disputes. It has no general sovereignty over the Moon and no inherent plenary power over residents or workers.

CODA's institutional power accumulates through contractual dependence and administrative practice. The narrative should not portray it as a world government or sovereign lunar state.

## Tycho Charter: proposed legal scope

The proposed Charter codifies five core protections:

1. **Bodily autonomy:** Employers cannot compel augmentation, deny medically necessary treatment, or remotely disable medical systems as discipline.
2. **Medical data rights:** Neural telemetry and physiological information receive medical privacy protections even when collected by employer-owned equipment.
3. **Habitat safety:** Life-support systems are protected infrastructure; labor disputes cannot justify punitive resource restrictions.
4. **Employment jurisdiction:** Contracts cannot waive basic legal protections or remove independent courts/tribunals.
5. **Representation:** Permanent off-world workers receive formal representation in CODA governance and safety oversight.

The Charter does not dissolve CODA, grant lunar independence, eliminate terrestrial national jurisdictions, nationalize private infrastructure, or end corporate ownership of industrial equipment. It establishes that people working in cislunar infrastructure cannot be treated as components of that infrastructure.

## Maya's tribunal theory: proposed

Maya's argument distinguishes CODA's legitimacy from the legality of the particular consolidation mandate:

- The 2035 emergency arrangements were limited to specified infrastructure/procurement measures; they did not authorize unlimited control over workers' bodies, medical information, or political rights.
- Later contracts and administrative practice exceeded their original purpose. David's licensing schedules blurred the boundary between proprietary industrial diagnostics and medically relevant information.
- CODA's authority is delegated, not inherent. Participating states and operators cannot grant it powers they do not possess or use delegation to evade their own legal obligations.
- Maya does not need to prove CODA was illegally founded. She needs to show that the current interpretation exceeds the legal basis on which CODA was established.
- The proposed tribunal ruling is narrow: CODA lacks delegated jurisdiction to enforce the specific consolidation mandate. CODA survives and the parties negotiate reforms.

## Revised 14-scene outline

### Act I — The Long Erosion (2026–2040)

**Scene 1: The Lake House (Thanksgiving 2026 — Presque Isle County, Northern Michigan)**  
The Mercers gather at Arthur and Helen's cabin. Arthur celebrates municipal approval for a Columbus grid and water-main modernization project. Claire, a junior trade lawyer in Washington, warns that transformer and sensor supply chains are exposed to regional export controls. David demonstrates a rudimentary, non-invasive cognitive-assist headband, calibrated to translate basic cursor intent. Four-year-old Maya tries it under supervision while one-year-old Leo plays nearby. Establishes the family baseline: Arthur builds public systems, Claire tracks geopolitical friction, and David solves technical interface problems.

**Scene 2: The Municipal Easement (Spring 2028 — Columbus, Ohio)**  
Supply delays and municipal bond caps threaten Arthur's grid upgrade. He drafts a public-private operating easement delegating maintenance to a regional utility trust. He includes quarterly public audits, city inspection access, strict rate caps, and a ten-year legislative renewal requirement. He files annotated drafts in his home study. The first link in the chain is a responsible, well-protected compromise—not negligence.

**Scene 3: The Winter Crunch (February 2035 — Washington, D.C.)**  
A severe regional winter grid failure and component shortage disrupts freight and power across the Great Lakes and Northeast. Claire helps draft narrow, regional emergency procurement and continuity waivers so contractors can bypass standard bidding to restore substations. Drawing on municipal trust templates like Arthur's, she restricts the waivers to eighteen months and mandates post-emergency legislative review. This remains a regional stress test, not an early global collapse.

**Scene 4: The Sunday Dinner Argument (Autumn 2036 — Columbus, Ohio)**  
Arthur reads an infrastructure law review noting that Claire's 2035 waiver language is being copied into long-term federal energy contracts with sunset clauses quietly dropped. He warns that emergency exceptions become permanent when balance sheets depend on them. Claire argues that rigid procedures would have left hospitals without heat. Fourteen-year-old Maya listens from the hall.

### Act II — The Rupture and Reconstruction (2041–2059)

**Scene 5: The Licensing Pivot (2044 — Denver, Colorado)**  
Three years into the post-2041 geopolitical divergence, commercial funding tightens. To keep his neural-interface firm alive, David signs a defense-industrial contract adapting his control software for remote heavy-machinery and orbital operations. At procurement lawyers' insistence, an IP licensing schedule defines interface feedback and latency telemetry as proprietary system diagnostics. David does not consider how that boundary may blur once interfaces become implanted medical-industrial hybrids.

**Scene 6: Ceasefire and Geneva (November 2054–January 2055 — Columbus and Geneva)**  
Following the outbreak of WWIII in November 2051, David's remote-operation firmware is integrated into orbital defense and logistics networks. He confronts how little control he has over the deployment of his work; he is not responsible for or involved in the Japanese attack that triggered the war. The war winds down in distinct stages: November 22, 2054 ceasefire; January 2, 2055 Turkish surrender; January 14, 2055 Treaty of Geneva. Eighty-six-year-old Arthur watches the signing from Columbus; Claire joins postwar reconstruction task forces in Switzerland.

**Scene 7: The Infrastructure Cooperation Agreement (2057 — Geneva)**  
Separate from the 2057 Turkey peace negotiations, major powers and aerospace operators negotiate the Infrastructure Cooperation Agreement to finance and standardize orbital and lunar rebuilding. Private consortia seek long-term operational predictability; states refuse to cede sovereignty. Claire adapts the logic of her 2035 delegated-authority framework into a contractual coordinating model explicitly bounded by national law and public oversight.

**Scene 8: The Passing of the Guard (2059–2061 — Columbus, Ohio)**  
Following CODA's proposed formal establishment in 2058–2059, Arthur dies in Columbus in 2061 at age ninety-three. Maya, now thirty-nine and an established senior counsel in transboundary and cislunar logistics, returns from Geneva for the funeral. Helping Claire pack Arthur's study, Maya takes custody of his indexed municipal binders. In 2063, when Claire retires to Maine, she sends Maya her own legal drafting files. Helen's earlier correspondence and household records help establish the family archival habit.

### Act III — The Architecture of Tomorrow (2062–2066)

**Scene 9: The Shackleton Rim (2062 — The Moon)**  
Thirty-seven-year-old Leo works as an augmented structural technician at Shackleton Rim. An implant calibration issue causes chronic migraines. He discovers he cannot export his own neural telemetry to an independent terrestrial neurologist: under consortium licensing rules derived from 2040s industrial IP schedules, the data stream is treated as proprietary equipment diagnostics and maintenance is restricted to employer-authorized technicians.

**Scene 10: The Throttling Incident (Early 2065 — Shackleton Rim)**  
Inspired by the terrestrial Augment Rights Movement, Shackleton technicians organize a work slowdown demanding independent medical audits and contract reform. The operating consortium invokes emergency operational authority to consolidate habitat life-support, communications, and maintenance under centralized administrative control. During the standoff, an automated load-balancing protocol throttles thermal and oxygen reserves to the technicians' residential module to prioritize industrial power. Leo leads an emergency manual override, preventing fatalities and becoming the reluctant spokesperson for the workers. The event should arise from institutional mismanagement, not cartoon villainy or an assumed deliberate murder attempt.

**Scene 11: The Paper Trail (Summer 2065 — Geneva)**  
As Senior Legal Counsel in CODA's Institutional Governance Division, forty-three-year-old Maya is assigned to draft the legal justification defending the consortium's consolidation mandate, partly because she helped streamline CODA logistics contracts. Reviewing the cited precedents, she recognizes language lifted from Claire's 2057 and 2035 agreements and IP schedules resembling David's 2044 contracts. She retrieves Arthur's 2028 municipal easement and Claire's files. Comparing originals to modern descendants, she sees that Arthur's inspection clauses, Claire's sunset limits, and David's narrow diagnostic scope have been stripped away or reinterpreted. Her realization is initially professional skepticism, not instant certainty.

**Scene 12: Two Conversations (Autumn 2065 — Maine Coast and Lagrange Point 1)**  
Maya visits Claire in Maine. Claire warns that exposing CODA's contractual vulnerabilities could create a jurisdictional vacuum and invite militarization of cislunar space. Maya then travels to L1 to meet forty-year-old Leo, who shows her the physical reality of remote administrative control and insists that workers want fair rules, not the collapse of lunar infrastructure. Maya must reconcile Claire's institutional realism with Leo's demand for human dignity.

**Scene 13: The Geneva Tribunal (Winter 2065–2066 — Geneva)**  
Before the emergency CODA oversight tribunal, Maya breaks from the consortium's litigation strategy. Submitting unredacted 2035 drafting records and 2044 licensing schedules, she distinguishes CODA's legitimacy from this unlawful expansion of its mandate. The founding agreements did not authorize control over human medical data or punitive life-support restrictions; delegated authority cannot exceed its legal basis. The tribunal issues a narrow ruling: CODA lacks delegated jurisdiction to enforce the specific consolidation mandate.

**Scene 14: The Tycho Charter and the Lake (Late 2066 — Shackleton Rim and Northern Michigan)**  
With the consolidation mandate struck down, governments, operators, and worker delegates negotiate the proposed Tycho Charter, codifying bodily autonomy, medical-data privacy, protected life-support infrastructure, employment jurisdiction, and formal worker representation on CODA's safety council. CODA survives, reformed. Leo remains at Shackleton Rim as a safety-council delegate. Maya resigns from Geneva to teach institutional design and constitutional history. At the Michigan lake house at dusk, she watches a faint orbital relay cross the sky: ordinary, fragile infrastructure that future generations will still have to maintain.

## Scene 1 — The Lake House

*Thanksgiving Day — November 26, 2026*  
*Presque Isle County, Northern Michigan*

The first real snow of the season had fallen overnight, three inches of heavy, wet lake-effect powder that clung to the white pines and muffled the lap of Lake Huron against the cedar dock.

Inside the cabin, the radiators clanked with the steady, metallic rhythm Arthur Mercer had spent forty years trusting. The living room smelled of woodsmoke, roasting turkey, and damp wool. On the television in the corner, muted beneath the chatter of the kitchen, a cable news ticker scrolled past headlines about semiconductor export restrictions in the Pacific, European energy subsidies, and another looming congressional continuing resolution. Nobody in the room was watching it.

Arthur, fifty-eight, stood at the dining table with his reading glasses perched low on his nose, smoothing out a rolled set of municipal blueprints over the tablecloth before Claire could set down the gravy boat.

"Careful with the corner there, sweetheart," Arthur said, weighting the edge of the vellum with a salt shaker. "City council signed off on the bond issue Tuesday afternoon. Phase one starts in March. Forty-two miles of water trunk mains and three modernized electrical substations for the Scioto corridor."

Claire paused, gravy boat in hand, looking down at the neat blue grid lines. At twenty-eight, she still carried the brisk, slightly guarded posture of a junior associate surviving her second year at a Washington trade practice. "That’s great, Dad. Truly. Though I hope your contractors already have the step-down transformers in a warehouse in Ohio."

Arthur frowned, peering over his rims. "We’re issuing the RFPs next month. Standard municipal procurement."

"Standard procurement assumes standard lead times," Claire said, setting the gravy boat down safely away from the blueprints. "Half the grain-oriented electrical steel and high-voltage switchgear for projects like that comes through three ports in East Asia. Two of my clients just had industrial shipments held up for six weeks over customs reclassifications. Everything is getting treated like a dual-use security asset now."

"It’s a water-main and substation upgrade for Columbus, Claire, not a missile silo," Arthur said, rolling the blueprints back up with practiced, unhurried precision. "You spend too much time in D.C. reading worst-case memos. Supply chains bend, prices go up a few points, and then manufacturers adjust. That’s how markets work. People still need clean water and lights that turn on."

"Until the people making the switches decide they need them more than we do," Claire murmured, though she softened it with a smile and reached over to squeeze his shoulder. "Come on. Mom’s going to disown both of us if the potatoes get cold."

Across the room by the stone hearth, David wasn't paying attention to the trade debate. At twenty-four, wearing a faded flannel shirt with the sleeves pushed past his elbows, he was kneeling on the braided rug beside a small Pelican case open to reveal a tangle of ribbon cables, an optical sensor bar, and a lightweight gray headband lined with dry-contact electrodes. He had spent ten minutes before dinner running a baseline calibration routine so the unit could filter out ambient noise.

Four-year-old Maya sat cross-legged in front of him, her chin propped in her hands, watching intently as David adjusted the strap behind her ears. A few feet away on a folded quilt, one-year-old Leo chewed methodically on the corner of a wooden block, occasionally glancing up at his older sister.

"Okay, Maya, remember—don't scrunch your eyebrows," David said, tapping a sequence into his tablet. "Just look at the blue square on the screen and think about pushing it to the right."

Maya stared at the tablet with ferocious concentration, her small brow immediately furrowing. On the screen, a white cursor jittered, drifted half an inch, and stopped.

"It's stuck, Uncle David," she said seriously.

"That’s because you’re trying to move it with your forehead instead of your motor signals," David laughed, recalibrating the gain slider on his tablet. "It’s still clunky. Latency’s around three hundred milliseconds, and if you blink too hard it reads a false click. Give it another try—easy this time."

Maya relaxed her face. Slowly, haltingly, the cursor slid across the glass and bumped into the blue square, turning it green. Maya let out a delighted gasp, clapping her hands, which immediately sent the cursor skittering wildly across the display into a cascade of red error bars.

"See?" David grinned, lifting the headband gently off her hair and powering down the unit. "Still basically a parlor trick. Right now it’s just translating surface intent into simple directional commands. Ten years from now, maybe it helps a surgeon steady a tremor or lets an engineer run diagnostics hands-free without looking at a keyboard."

"As long as it doesn't require an act of Congress to get the replacement batteries," Arthur called from the head of the table, carving knife poised over the turkey. "Everyone sit. Food’s on."

They gathered around the oak table—Arthur and his wife, Helen; Claire; David; and the two children, Maya boosted up on two thick catalog volumes while Leo hammered his spoon against his high-chair tray. Outside the wide front window, twilight was settling over Lake Huron, turning the water the color of slate.

Arthur bowed his head briefly, offered a short, familiar grace thanking God for family, steady work, and another year together, and began filling plates.

Around the table, the conversation drifted easily from winterizing the cabin's pipes to Claire’s upcoming holiday travel and David’s shoestring startup budget. The house was warm, the radiator pipes ticked steadily against the frost, and beyond the glass the lake stretched out into the dusk—vast, quiet, and solid beneath the early ice.
