# Scene 2: The Municipal Easement

> **Status: NON-CANON / DIFF PROPOSAL ONLY**  
> Part of *The Inheritance* narrative development packet. Scene text is preserved as supplied; it does not amend setting canon.

*Spring — April 18, 2028*  
*Columbus, Ohio*

The rain had been falling against the fourth-floor windows of the Columbus Department of Public Utilities since dawn, blurring the Scioto River into a ribbon of slate gray.

Inside Conference Room B, Arthur Mercer—sixty now, his hair thinner at the temples than it had been at the lake house two Novembers ago—spread the revised Scioto Corridor procurement ledger across the laminate table. Beside him sat Marcus Vance, Deputy Director of Municipal Finance, nursing a cold cup of coffee, and across from them sat two representatives from Great Lakes Grid & Water Partners, a regional infrastructure consortium backed by Midwestern pension funds and private equity.

Claire had been right about the transformers.

Lead times on grain-oriented electrical steel had stretched from fourteen months to thirty-two. Worse, municipal bond yields had jumped following consecutive federal budget standoffs, leaving the city’s Phase One capital allocation nearly nineteen million dollars short before a single trench was dug along the water trunk line.

"If we re-bid the substation switchgear under standard municipal rules," Arthur said, tapping a red-ink column on the sheet, "we lose the current manufacturing slot in Toledo and push completion past 2032. Two of the existing relays on the south loop are already running past rated load."

"Which is why our proposal solves your capital gap," said Elena Rostova, Great Lakes’ lead counsel, sliding a bound draft agreement across the table. "We front the nineteen million, absorb the supply-chain escalation on the step-down transformers, and take over operational maintenance of the forty-two-mile trunk line and the three substations under a thirty-year operating easement."

Arthur picked up the draft, adjusting his reading glasses. He had spent the past three weeks marking up earlier versions in the evenings at his dining room table, while Helen sorted school-library catalog cards across from him.

"Thirty years is a long lease on public water and power," Arthur said evenly. "And your initial draft gave Great Lakes unilateral authority over emergency rate adjustments and third-party subcontractor assignments."

"Because we’re carrying the financing risk, Mr. Mercer," Rostova replied, not unkindly. "No institutional lender will underwrite municipal infrastructure if every maintenance decision has to wait six months for a city council subcommittee."

"I understand operational speed," Arthur said. "I’ve spent thirty-five years trying to get crews out before pipes freeze. What I don't accept is handing over public utility infrastructure without a leash."

He reached into his leather briefcase and pulled out a crisp, twelve-page rider clipped to his own annotated copy of the agreement.

"Section Fourteen," Arthur said, setting copies in front of Vance and the consortium lawyers. "You get operational management and a fixed-margin service fee. In return, four conditions are non-negotiable. First: quarterly public telemetry and maintenance disclosures filed directly with the City Clerk—no proprietary black boxes on water pressure, load balancing, or repair backlogs. Second: unannounced municipal engineering inspection rights on forty-eight hours' notice. Third: rate-cap indexing tied strictly to regional CPI, requiring an affirmative council vote for any variance. Fourth: a mandatory ten-year legislative renewal review. If the consortium fails performance benchmarks at year ten, the easement reverts to the city at depreciated asset value."

Rostova frowned, flipping through Arthur’s rider. Her colleague leaned over to whisper something about investor liquidity.

Marcus Vance cleared his throat, glancing nervously at Arthur. "Arthur, if they walk away, we don't have a backup plan before winter."

Arthur didn't blink. He looked steadily across the table at Rostova. "You want stable, guaranteed municipal cash flow for a decade, and you get it. But this city built these lines with public money for public residents. If you want to run them, you run them in daylight."

Silence held the room for a long moment, broken only by the steady patter of rain against the glass. Finally, Rostova exhaled, uncapped her pen, and made a quick note in the margin of Section Fourteen.

"We’ll need a thirty-day cure period on any inspection deficiency before a renewal review can be triggered," she said. "And standard confidentiality exclusions for our proprietary diagnostic software."

Arthur paused, weighing the concession. A thirty-day cure period was standard engineering practice—fair enough to fix a faulty valve or relay without triggering a legal fight. And diagnostic software meant the consortium's internal scheduling tools, not the physical grid metrics.

"Thirty days to cure, documented in writing," Arthur agreed. "And the confidentiality exclusion applies strictly to internal administrative software—never to physical system performance or safety logs."

"Done," Rostova said, extending her hand across the table.

---

That evening, the rain had cleared to a cool spring dusk. Arthur sat in his study on the second floor of the Columbus house, the desk lamp casting a warm circle over the signed execution copy of the Scioto Corridor Operating Easement.

Helen appeared in the doorway carrying two mugs of chamomile tea, wearing her reading cardigan. She glanced at the thick bound contract beside his slide rule and rolled blueprints.

"You look like a man who just traded away half the city," she said softly, setting a mug beside his elbow.

"Just an operating lease," Arthur sighed, rubbing the bridge of his nose. "Good terms, too. Tighter oversight than half the state highway contracts. Still—it’s the first time we’ve handed routine grid maintenance outside the department."

Helen picked up his heavily marked-up working drafts—the pages covered in Arthur’s neat architectural block printing and red-ink revisions—and squared the edges together with practiced, librarian precision.

"Then don't throw away the drafts," she said, pulling an empty archival binder from the bookshelf behind him and slipping the annotated pages inside behind a dated tab. "People forget why rules were written once the emergency passes. Keep the receipts, Arthur."

Arthur smiled faintly, taking a sip of tea as Helen labeled the spine *SCIOTO CORRIDOR EASEMENT — 1928–2028 ARCHIVE / PUBLIC OVERSIGHT RIDERS*, and slid it onto the shelf beside her own boxes of family letters and photographs.
