# New Korean Wave

## Preserved source

```wikitext
The '''New Korean Wave''' was the name given to a period of popularity enjoyed by Korean art, music, films, and TV series, stretching roughly from the unification of North and South Korea to [World War III](107-world-war-iii.md). The name was taken from the original "Korean Wave", a trend for South Korean popular culture, especially pop music or "K-pop", that was prominent in the late 1990s, 2000s, and 2010s. Most historians of consumer culture mark the reunification of Korea in 2031 as the beginning point of the New Korean Wave, as it greatly expanded Korea's (formerly South Korea) presence in the economy in east Asia.

The New Korean Wave was marked by an increased foreign market for the Korean [car](https://second-renaissance.fandom.com/wiki/car), robotics, and electronics industries-- all fields that had previously been dominated by the neighboring country of [Japan](949-japan.md). As relationships between Korea and Japan began to deteriorate over the course of the 2030s and into the 2040s, the United States actually leaned on Korea to expand its global economy as a "counter" to Japan. In fact, it could be said that by the 2040s, Korea's role in the global economy more or less mirrored that of Japan 40 years previously.

The New Korean Wave came to an abrupt end with Japan's invasion of the Korean Peninsula. With Korea's government now devoted to military production, the output of cultural exports that had become associated with the country during the previous two decades were either stopped completely or (where possible) redirected to producing war-related goods. However, its repercussions were still felt during the war. US President [Evalyn Jacobi](317-evalyn-jacobi.md) came up with the "Korean Ties" campaign, which essentially encouraged Americans to contribute economically to the war effort by purchasing Korean goods. This did not pass without comment from many Japanophiles, who saw it as a thinly veiled attack on their lifestyle (though others, it should be noted, did not mind it at all, as they were just as interested in Korean exports).

After the war ended, Korea's shattered economy was not able to rebuild itself to the heights of the 2030s and 2040s, and the 2050s are generally accepted as the end point of the New Korean Wave. While Korea remained a close economic partner of the United States for the rest of its history, and the after-effects of the New Korean Wave would continue to be felt in both countries' popular culture, this unique period would remain consigned to the early 21st century.
```
