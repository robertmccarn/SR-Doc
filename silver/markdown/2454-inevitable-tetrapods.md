# Inevitable Tetrapods

## Preserved source

```wikitext
'''''Inevitable Tetrapods '''''was a composition of various theories attempting to explain the convergent evolution of tetrapod-esque (four-limbed) body plans on planets with complex animal-like life. The book's title is something of a misnomer, as it does acknowledge the existence of alien species with more than four limbs. Its main thesis, however, is that the evolution of four limbs is a "path of least resistance" for vertebrate-like organisms evolving a land-dwelling lifestyle, at least under Earth-like circumstances. The fewer limbs an organism has, so the argument goes, the less complex its movement, ergo the fewer resources it needs to consume.

Furthermore, the majority of land vertebrate-analogs on alien worlds were, like tetrapods on Earth, descended from fish-like swimmers (though they in turn often had ancestors that varied considerably--the first "vertebrates" on [Tu](1668-tu.md), for example, resembled velvet worms, but these later evolved a fishlike form). The fluidity of water, rather than other environmental conditions such as atmosphere composition or gravity, dictates the form of a swimming creature, so these swimmers often ended up looking superficially similar to fish on Earth, with torpedo-shaped bodies and fins. Such body shapes, the writers argued, were predisposed to evolving tetrapod-like forms when colonizing land.

Non-tetrapodal aliens, such as the [Velminyak](2622-velminyak.md), the [Tel-bn](1145-tel-bn.md), and the [Constructors](2589-constructors.md), seem to have primarily evolved on worlds where the common ancestor of the local land "vertebrates" were not fish-like. The Tel-bn evolved from amphibious squid-like creatures, the Velminyak from something akin to polychaetes, and the Constructors from creatures resembling starfish.

The book was well-received in the exobiological community, with biologist Lauren Naish going as far as to declare it "the best treatise ever written on convergent evolution and the forces that drive it," and calling it "a must-have for any lover of tetrapod zoology."
```
